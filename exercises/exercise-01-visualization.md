## Overview

In this exercise you will explore the PReSto visualization platform using published paleoclimate reconstructions that are already loaded and ready to view. The goal is to get comfortable with the interface — navigating time periods, switching between reconstructions, and reading spatial and temporal structure — before you start building your own reconstructions tomorrow.

There is no coding in this exercise. You will work directly in your browser, capture screenshots or save figures, and upload them to our shared slide deck so the group can compare results at the end.

## Key Platform Features to Know

Before diving into the tasks, take a few minutes to locate these controls in the visualization platform:

- **Reconstruction selector** (top dropdown): switches between published reconstructions and methods
- **Variable selector**: choose the climate variable you want to display (e.g., surface air temperature)
- **Time slider**: scrub through time or type a specific year/decade
- **Time series panel**: click any grid cell on the map to pull up a time series for that location
- **Download button**: export the full reconstruction as a NetCDF file

## Task 1: Holocene Comparison (~25 minutes)

Many things about the early Holocene remain contested. Two published reconstructions — the **Last Glacial Maximum Reanalysis (LGMR)** and the **Holocene Data Assimilation product (HoloDA, Michael Erb et al.)** — take quite different approaches and produce strikingly different spatial patterns in the early Holocene, particularly around 8,000–6,000 years before present.

### Steps

1. Open the PReSto visualization platform: [paleopresto.org](https://paleopresto.org/)
2. Select the **LGMR** reconstruction. Set the variable to **surface air temperature**.
3. Navigate to approximately **6,000 years BP**. Take a screenshot of the global map.
4. Click on a grid cell in the **North Atlantic / Laurentide region** and screenshot the time series panel.
5. Switch to the **HoloDA** reconstruction. Navigate to the same time period.
6. Take the same two screenshots (map + time series for the same grid cell).
7. Upload all four screenshots to your slide. Add a sentence or two describing the most striking difference you notice between the two reconstructions.

> **Discussion prompt:** The LGMR shows a pronounced cold anomaly in the North Atlantic in the early Holocene that fades through time. Why might that be? What do you think is driving the difference between the two products?

## Task 2: A Volcanic Eruption (~25 minutes)

The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora is one of the largest in the historical record and produced a well-documented global cooling signal. Here you will examine how that signal appears across different Common Era reconstructions.

### Steps

1. Select the **LMR 2.1** reconstruction. Set the variable to **surface air temperature**.
2. Navigate to **1816** (the "Year Without a Summer").
3. Screenshot the global anomaly map.
4. Click on a grid cell in **central North America** and screenshot the time series, zooming in to the 1800–1830 window.
5. Switch to at least one other Common Era reconstruction (e.g., **PAGES 2k**) and repeat steps 3–4 for the same locations.
6. Upload your screenshots and add a brief note: does the spatial structure of the cooling look the same across reconstructions? Where does it differ?

> **Discussion prompt:** What would cause two reconstructions to agree on the timing of Tambora cooling but disagree on its spatial extent or magnitude?

## Task 3: Choose Your Own Event (~30 minutes)

Pick a climate event or period you are personally interested in — it can be anything covered by one of the available reconstructions. Some ideas if you need a starting point:

- Younger Dryas (~12,900–11,700 BP)
- Medieval Climate Anomaly (~950–1250 CE)
- Little Ice Age (~1300–1850 CE)
- 8.2 ka event
- A specific historical drought or flood event

### Steps

1. Choose a reconstruction and time period relevant to your event.
2. Explore the spatial and temporal structure of the climate signal.
3. Capture 2–3 screenshots that best illustrate what you found.
4. Upload to your slide with a title and a short description: what event did you pick, what reconstruction did you use, and what does the spatial/temporal structure look like?

We'll briefly share these out at the end of the exercise — be ready to spend ~2 minutes walking the group through what you found.
